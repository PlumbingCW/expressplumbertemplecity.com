Plumbing for a Garbage Disposal in a Temple City Kitchen

A garbage disposal needs a drain that can take it, a dishwasher connection done correctly and a safe power supply. This guide walks through what the plumbing involves, where installs go wrong and what to check in an older Temple City kitchen.

By the Temple City Express Plumbing team · November 13, 2026

24/7 Emergency Line Open

What a disposal connects to

A disposal sits under the sink and bolts to the sink drain opening. It has three connections to know about. The top connects to the sink flange, which is sealed with putty or a gasket. The side outlet connects to the drain trap and the waste line. A second small inlet on the side, if present, takes the dishwasher drain hose.

The unit also needs power, through a switch on the wall or an air switch on the counter, and an outlet or a hardwired connection under the sink. The plumbing and the wiring are separate jobs, and an electrician may do the wiring, depending on your house.

The dishwasher connection

A new disposal has a plug in the dishwasher inlet, and it must be knocked out with a hammer and screwdriver before the dishwasher hose is connected. Forgetting this step is the most common installation mistake, and the dishwasher then fills with water and will not drain.

The dishwasher drain hose also needs to rise to the underside of the countertop in a high loop, or run through an air gap, so that sink water cannot flow back into the dishwasher. Follow the dishwasher manual and local code, which can specify one or the other.

Under the California Plumbing Code, which is based on the Uniform Plumbing Code (UPC), the disposal needs a trap, and the dishwasher drain needs a high loop or air gap.

Drain size, the trap and slope

Kitchen drains are normally one and a half inches. A disposal sends a lot of food waste into that pipe, and the pipe has to carry it away. The trap must be below the disposal and connect to the wall with a gentle downward slope. Too little slope and food waste settles, too much and water outruns the solids.

Choosing a size

Disposals range from about a third of a horsepower to one horsepower or more. A larger motor chews faster and jams less, and it costs more. For a household that cooks often, a half horsepower or larger is a sensible choice, and a continuous-feed model is the usual type.

Another choice is how the disposal mounts. Some mount with a simple twist-lock, which makes replacement easier, and others use a fixed ring that takes more time to remove. If your old unit has leaked, check the cabinet floor and the shutoffs too. We cover that in our faucet and valve repair service, and the disposal itself is handled under garbage disposal repair.

Older kitchens in Temple City

Houses built in the 1950s make up 30.6 percent of Temple City's housing. Kitchen drains in those houses are often original or partly replaced, and they may have scale and grease on their walls. Adding a disposal sends more material down a pipe that may already be narrowed, so a camera look before you install one is cheap insurance.

Water hardness plays a role too. Golden State Water's South San Gabriel system averaged about 240 mg/L in 2025, and scale can build on seals and the grinding surfaces. Rinse with cold water while the unit runs, and clean the trap if the smell returns.

Temple City's municipal code adopts the 2025 California Plumbing Code, and permit rules vary by job, so ask the Temple City building department. If you live in the neighboring cities, our pages for El Monte and Rosemead describe the older housing stock there.

What to put in and what to keep out

A disposal is meant for soft food scraps. Keep out grease, bones, fibrous peels, large amounts of coffee grounds and anything that expands in water, such as rice and pasta. Never put a hand in the disposal, and never use chemical drain cleaners in it.

If the sink drains slowly even with the disposal clear, the problem is further down the line. Our drain cleaning page covers that.

Wiring and switch options

Disposals run on household power. Some come with a cord that plugs into a switched outlet under the sink. Others are hardwired into a junction box. The switch can be a wall switch, an air switch on the countertop or a switch built into the cabinet.

If your sink has a switch but no outlet under it, an electrician may need to add one. Do not modify wiring if you are not qualified. A disposal on a circuit shared with a dishwasher or other loads can trip the breaker, and a dedicated circuit is better.

Make sure power is off at the breaker before you work, because the switch alone does not isolate the circuit.

A first-day checklist after the install

  • ✓Run cold water into the sink and check every joint under the cabinet for drips.
  • ✓Run the dishwasher through a cycle and watch for water backing up into the sink.
  • ✓Switch the disposal on with water running and listen for rattles or scraping.
  • ✓Check the flange for a seal at the top, from inside the cabinet.
  • ✓Dry the cabinet floor and look again after an hour.

A small drip on day one is easier to fix than a rotted cabinet a year later. If anything looks off, call us before it gets worse.
