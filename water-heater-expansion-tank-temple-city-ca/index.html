Water Heater Expansion Tank Installation in Temple City

When a water heater warms its water, the water expands, and in a closed plumbing system it has nowhere to go. An expansion tank absorbs it. This guide explains how to tell whether you need one, how to install it and what to watch for.

By the Temple City Express Plumbing team · November 13, 2026

24/7 Emergency Line Open

Why water heaters need room to expand

Water expands as it heats. A 50 gallon tank heated by about 70 degrees Fahrenheit grows by roughly a gallon. In an open system, the extra water flows back into the street supply. In a closed system, it cannot.

A system becomes closed when there is a check valve, a backflow preventer or a pressure regulator with a check feature on the supply line. Many houses have one. In a closed system, the expanding water raises pressure inside the heater and the pipes, which stresses fittings, shortens the life of the tank and makes the relief valve drip.

Signs your system needs an expansion tank

  • ✓The temperature and pressure relief valve drips or weeps after a heating cycle.
  • ✓Banging in the pipes when the heater fires.
  • ✓Static pressure that jumps higher when the heater is on.
  • ✓Faucets or toilet fill valves that fail more often than they should.
  • ✓A heater that has been replaced after a short life.

A gauge screwed onto an outside hose bib, read with the house quiet, shows static pressure. Read it again after the water heater has run. A big rise is the sign.

The California Plumbing Code, which is based on the Uniform Plumbing Code (UPC), addresses thermal expansion in closed water systems, which is why an expansion tank is a code topic and not just an accessory.

Sizing and choosing a tank

Most residential expansion tanks hold about two gallons, which suits common 40 to 50 gallon water heaters. Larger heaters and higher pressure call for bigger tanks. The tank has an air charge in a bladder, and that charge must match your static pressure before it is installed.

How installation goes

  1. 01

    Shut down

    Turn off power or gas, close the cold supply and drain the pipe near the heater.

  2. 02

    Set the air charge

    Measure house pressure and set the tank's air charge to match.

  3. 03

    Connect

    Add a tee on the cold line near the heater and thread the tank on with sealant.

  4. 04

    Support and test

    Support the tank, restore water, bleed air and check every joint for leaks.

A tank installed with a charge that is too low or too high does little. Setting it right is the part that matters, and it is also the part people skip. If you want it done with the heater swap, ask about it when you plan a water heater installation.

Expansion tanks and relief valves

A relief valve is the last safety device on a water heater. It is designed to open if pressure or temperature gets dangerous. A valve that drips after each heating cycle may simply be reacting to thermal expansion, and replacing the valve will not stop it. Fix the expansion, and the dripping usually stops.

If a valve runs steadily, the heater may be overheating or the pressure may be too high from the street. Both need attention, and our water heater repair page explains what we check.

Maintaining an expansion tank

An expansion tank is a simple device, but it does not last forever. Inside is a rubber diaphragm or bladder that separates air from water. Over time the bladder can fail, or the air charge can leak down. A failed tank fills with water, feels heavy and makes no difference to pressure.

Check it once a year. Tap the tank with your knuckle. The top should sound hollow, with an air-filled ring, and the bottom should sound solid. Press the air valve, as you would on a tire, and a hiss of air is good. A spray of water means the bladder has failed and the tank needs replacing.

Shut off the water and drain the tank before checking the air charge. Never check it with the tank under pressure.

Common mistakes with expansion tanks

  • ✓Installing a tank without setting its air charge to the house pressure.
  • ✓Mounting a heavy tank on a copper pipe with no support, which can crack the joint.
  • ✓Fitting a tank on the hot side, where the heat shortens its life.
  • ✓Replacing a relief valve over and over without fixing the thermal expansion.
  • ✓Forgetting to look at the pressure regulator, which may be failing and sending high pressure into the house.

If you are unsure whether a tank is already installed, look near the heater for a small blue or gray vessel on the cold line. Our water heater repair page covers what we check when we visit.
