When a hidden leak is an emergency

Some leaks can wait for a daytime visit. A drip from a faucet is one. Others cannot. Water coming through a ceiling, a wet wall that is getting bigger, warm spots on a floor, or a meter that spins with every tap closed are all signs that water is escaping now.

Before we arrive, close the main valve, shut off breakers for any area where water is near outlets, and put a bucket under active drips. Photograph the damage and note where water first appeared, because that tells the plumber where to start looking.

We take these calls at night, on weekends and on holidays, and same-day appointments are available when we have an opening.

Meter still running or a high water bill

A water bill that has jumped with no change in how the house is used is one of the most common reasons people call. You can run a simple test yourself.

  1. Turn off every faucet, the washing machine and the dishwasher, and make sure no one flushes.
  2. Open the meter box, usually at the curb, and look at the low-flow indicator. If it is turning, water is moving.
  3. Note the reading, wait fifteen minutes with everything still off, and read it again.
  4. If the number changed, close your house main valve and check the meter once more. If it stops, the leak is on your side of the meter.

If the meter keeps moving with the house main closed, the problem may be between the meter and the valve, which is where water service line repair comes in.

Water utilities generally publish guidelines for checking your own meter for leaks, and a moving dial with every fixture off is the signal they describe.

How we find the leak without opening everything

We start by listening. Electronic listening gear picks up the hiss of pressurized water in a pipe and tells the plumber which wall, floor or yard section to examine. A thermal camera shows cool or warm patches on a surface, and a moisture meter confirms them.

When the leak is on a hot or cold line, we may isolate sections with a pressure test. The goal is a short list of spots, so any opening is small and in the right place. We explain what we found before anyone cuts anything.

Where leaks hide in Temple City houses

Ceiling and wall leaks show up first as a brown stain, a bubbled paint patch or a musty smell. They are usually a supply line or a drain above, and a bathroom over a kitchen is a classic place for one. Under-house and crawlspace leaks are quieter, often found by a damp smell or a floor that feels warm or soft.

Buried lines carry water from the meter to the house, and irrigation lines run through most yards. A wet patch of lawn in dry weather, or a pool that loses water faster than evaporation explains, suggests a line is leaking underground. Pool and spa leaks follow the same method, with the pool's own plumbing tested separately. A warm spot on a floor usually means a hot line running under the slab, which is the territory of slab leak repair.

Why older homes in Temple City leak where they do

Temple City's housing element reports that 57.7 percent of homes are over 60 years old, and 30.6 percent went up in the 1950s. In houses of that age, supply lines may be galvanized steel that has scaled shut, or copper that has developed pinholes from decades of water chemistry.

The water itself plays a part. Golden State Water's South San Gabriel system averaged about 240 mg/L of hardness in 2025, and scale in old pipes slows flow while corrosion thins the wall at the weakest points. That is why a house can leak in several places over a few years, and why we look at the condition of the whole line before recommending a spot repair.

When a house keeps developing leaks, a full repiping can cost less than chasing leaks one at a time, and we will say so after the survey.
