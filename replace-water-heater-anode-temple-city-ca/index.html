Replacing a Water Heater Anode Rod in a Temple City Home

A water heater anode rod corrodes so the steel tank does not, and when it is used up the tank starts to rust. This guide covers how to check it, which type to buy, how replacement goes and why water in Temple City makes the habit worthwhile.

By the Temple City Express Plumbing team · November 13, 2026

24/7 Emergency Line Open

What an anode rod does

Inside the tank of a water heater is a long metal rod, screwed into the top. It is called a sacrificial anode, and it does exactly what the name says. Because the rod is made of a more reactive metal than the steel tank, corrosion attacks the rod first and leaves the tank alone.

Once the rod is gone, the tank becomes the next target. Rust builds on the steel, and the lining eventually fails. That is why many water heater failures trace back to a rod nobody checked. Swapping a worn anode is far cheaper than replacing the heater, and on a younger tank it can add years.

Types of anode rods

Anode rod materials compared
TypeStrengthTrade-off
MagnesiumProtects well and is the common factory choiceCan wear fast in soft water, may give a sulfur smell

Aluminum and zincHelps reduce sulfur odorMixed results in some water
Powered anodeDoes not wear out the same wayNeeds electricity and costs more

Check your heater's manual before buying. Some warranties require a particular type or mention the rod, and a wrong choice can cause odor or poor protection.

How to check the rod

The rod hex head sits on top of the tank, often under a cap or the insulation. Checking is a short job, but it needs a large socket, often 1 1/16 inch, and a breaker bar, because the rod is usually tight.

  1. 01

    Power or gas off

    Turn off the breaker for an electric heater, or set a gas heater's control to off.

  2. 02

    Close the cold supply

    Shut the valve on the cold line to the tank.

  3. 03

    Relieve pressure

    Open a hot tap in the house and let the pressure drop.

  4. 04

    Pull and inspect

    Unscrew the rod, look at it and replace it if it is thin, pitted or has bare steel showing.

Steps for replacing the rod

Drain a few gallons from the tank first, so the water level is below the top. Loosen the rod and lift it out. If you do not have room above the tank, use an articulated rod, which bends so it can go in a tight space. Wrap the threads of the new rod with thread seal tape, thread it in and tighten.

Open the cold supply, open a hot tap until water runs clear, and then restore power or gas. Check the top for drips. If anything seeps, tighten gently. A water heater is full of hot water and pressure, so be careful and read the manual.

If the tank has already leaked from the bottom, the rod is no longer the issue, and our water heater replacement page covers what comes next. If you want a plumber to do the check and flush at the same time, our water heater repair service handles it.

How often to check

A reasonable habit is to look at the rod every few years, sooner in hard water. A magnesium rod in hard water may be half gone in three years. In soft water it may last longer or corrode quickly, depending on its type. If a heater is over six years old and the rod has never been checked, it is worth looking now.

While you are there, flush the tank. Open the drain valve with a hose attached and let a few gallons run until the water is clear. That removes the sediment that builds on the bottom of the tank.

What local water does to anodes

Temple City is served by more than one water system. Golden State Water's South San Gabriel system reported 2025 hardness from 189 to 310 mg/L, with an average of about 240 mg/L or 14 grains per gallon. Its South Arcadia system averaged 160 mg/L, about 9.4 grains. Hard water leaves scale and also affects how an anode wears.

That makes a yearly flush and a rod check a good habit, especially for a heater on the harder side. If you live near Arcadia or El Monte, the figures differ a little by supplier, as our pages for Arcadia and El Monte explain.

California Health and Safety Code 19211 requires water heaters to be braced, anchored or strapped, so check the straps whenever you are at the tank.

Noises, smells and rusty water: reading the signs

A heater can tell you its anode is gone before you pull it. Rust-colored hot water, with clear cold water at the same tap, suggests the tank is corroding inside. Popping or rumbling is usually scale on the bottom, which a flush helps. A sulfur or rotten egg smell in hot water often comes from bacteria reacting with a magnesium rod, and swapping to an aluminum or zinc-aluminum rod can reduce it.

None of these signs is proof on its own. A smell can also come from the water supply, and rust can come from old galvanized pipe and not from the tank. That is why pulling the rod is the real test.

If you find thick scale on the rod or on the floor of the tank when you drain it, plan on flushing the tank every year from now on.

Powered anodes and when they are worth the cost

A powered anode uses a small electric current to protect the tank, instead of a metal that wears away. Because nothing is consumed, it does not need to be replaced on a schedule. It costs more, it needs an outlet nearby and it suits a heater with an awkward top, where reaching a standard rod is hard.

For most Temple City homes a standard rod swapped every few years is the simple answer. A powered unit makes sense if the heater is in a tight closet, if the water is aggressive on rods or if you want to stop thinking about it. Check that the heater's warranty accepts the change before you buy one.

When to leave it to a plumber

If the rod will not turn, if there is no clearance above the heater, if the tank is already leaking or if the heater is gas and the vent is hard to reach, call us. A stuck rod can twist a fitting or damage the tank, and a gas heater adds safety checks. Call (626) 898-6169 to book a visit.
