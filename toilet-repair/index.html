Overflowing toilet: stop the water first

When a toilet is about to overflow, the fastest fix is at the tank. Lift the lid and hold the rubber flapper closed, or reach behind the bowl and turn the shutoff valve clockwise until it stops. Do not flush again.

Then put towels around the base, switch off any outlet that is near the water and call. We handle overflowing and leaking toilets at night, on weekends and on holidays, and same-day visits are available when we have an opening. If the water in the bowl is dark and smells of sewage, the problem may be in the drain line and not the toilet, which is covered on our drain cleaning page.

What is going wrong with the toilet

Most toilet problems fall into four groups. A running toilet usually has a worn flapper, a misadjusted fill valve or a chain that is too tight. A toilet that rocks or leaks at the floor needs a new wax ring or a repaired flange. A toilet that clogs often has a partial blockage in the trap or a weak flush. A toilet that sweats or drips from the tank has a loose bolt or a cracked tank.

Repair or replace the toilet

A toilet with a worn part is cheap to repair. One with a cracked bowl, a damaged tank or a poor flush that no adjustment improves is better replaced. A toilet that has been rebuilt several times is also a candidate, because each repair buys less time.

When we replace one, we set it on a new wax ring or an equivalent seal, connect it to a new supply line and check for leaks. If a repair reveals a damaged flange or a soft floor, we explain it before going further. For a broader remodel, the plumbing installation page covers fixtures and new lines.

Hard water and old flanges in Temple City toilets

Golden State Water's South San Gabriel system reported an average hardness of about 240 mg/L in 2025, about 14 grains per gallon. Mineral deposits collect on fill valves, flappers and rim jets, and a toilet that was working fine a few years ago can begin to run or flush weakly.

In houses from the 1950s, which make up 30.6 percent of Temple City's homes, the closet flange under the toilet is often cast iron or an old plastic that has cracked or rusted. If the toilet rocks after a reset, the flange is worth checking before a new ring goes on.

A routine check of the tank's inner parts every few years, with a drop of dye in the tank to test for silent leaks, is a cheap habit that prevents a surprise water bill.
